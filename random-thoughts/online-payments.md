A decade ago, paying online meant a multi-step form and a card number typed in by hand. Now I buy a filter coffee in Chennai by scanning a QR code, and it clears in seconds. That shift is worth sitting with: if payments moved this far in ten years, the next ten are unlikely to stand still.

This is a personal read, not a forecast — where I think each layer of the payments stack is heading, from the bank rails underneath to the crypto experiments at the edge. For how the pieces fit together today, see [the payment ecosystem](/citadel/interview/payment-ecosystem).

## The old rails: bank transfers and cards

Traditional bank transfers and card payments — the networks like Visa — still carry most of the world's money. Cross-border transfers have long meant SWIFT, a secure messaging network linking banks worldwide. It's robust, but slow and opaque by the standards of anything else we do online.

The rails are catching up. The US Federal Reserve launched FedNow, an instant-payment service that moves money between retail customers within seconds, around the clock, settling directly in central-bank accounts.

My take: traditional banking doesn't vanish, it sinks lower in the stack. These systems become the plumbing — less visible to the person paying, more critical than ever — and the visible innovation moves to the open banking APIs built on top.

## UPI and instant payments

India's Unified Payments Interface (UPI) has reshaped daily transactions here. Paying a friend, a street vendor, or an online bill is a QR scan or a virtual payment address, settled instantly, and any UPI app talks to any other. The interoperability is the part that stands out: no walled gardens.

My take: mobile-first, real-time, low-cost domestic transfers are a template other countries copy. Expect more national instant-payment schemes with the same shape, and eventually pressure to make them talk to each other across borders.

## Wallets and payment service providers

PayPal, Venmo, and — in India — Paytm became household names by hiding the mess of bank accounts and card numbers behind a clean interface. The underlying money movement is shifting too: from "pull" payments, where a merchant pulls funds from your account after you authorise a card, to "push" payments, where funds are sent straight to an account, as with Visa Direct.

My take: these players set the bar for user experience and are indispensable intermediaries for now. As the bank rails underneath get faster and more open, the open question is whether wallets stay in the payment path or move up to value-added services around it.

## Crypto and the decentralisation pitch

Any discussion of payments' future has to touch cryptocurrencies and blockchain. The original Bitcoin idea — a decentralised, peer-to-peer electronic cash system — is a genuine rethink of how a payment network could work, and books like *Mastering Bitcoin* lay out the mechanics well. The pitch is lower cross-border fees, faster settlement, and more transparency.

My take: the technology is sound, but everyday adoption faces real hurdles. Price volatility makes most people wary of spending crypto directly. Regulation is still forming, country by country. Scalability and the experience for non-technical users are unsolved. I expect crypto and traditional finance to converge rather than one replacing the other, with crypto arriving first where the alternative is worst: cross-border remittances, economies with unstable local currency, and native digital assets. For daily groceries, not in its current form.

## What else is coming

A few trends I'd bet on seeing more of:

- **Global instant payments.** More countries ship FedNow-style systems, and the harder next step is making them interoperable, which would simplify the foreign-exchange leg of a cross-border payment.
- **Embedded and invisible payments.** The transaction disappears into the app around it. Your Uber ride ends and the payment just happens.
- **AI in payments.** Fraud detection is the immediate use, followed by personalised payment flows and spending-linked financial advice.
- **Central bank digital currencies.** Digital versions of national currencies — centralised, so not cryptocurrency in the usual sense. How they interact with commercial banks and existing rails is the thing to watch.
- **Biometric payments.** Fingerprint, face, or palm. The technology exists; adoption tracks how secure and convenient it feels.
- **IoT payments.** Devices holding their own payment credentials — a car paying for its own charging, a fridge reordering groceries.

## The through-line

If there's a pattern, it's that the systems that win make the payment itself disappear — UPI, embedded checkout, tap-to-pay all pull the transaction into the background. The open question is who owns that invisible layer: the banks whose rails it runs on, the wallets that built the habit, or a central-bank digital currency that hasn't shipped yet.
