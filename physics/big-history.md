Read cosmic history as a sequence of **thresholds** — points where something genuinely new and more complex became possible — and one feature jumps out: the intervals between them collapse. Billions of years separate the Big Bang, the first stars, and the first planets. Life to intelligence is hundreds of millions of years; intelligence to agriculture, a few tens of thousands; agriculture to industry, a few thousand; industry to the digital age, about two hundred. The same process — complexity building on complexity — runs faster at every step.

The first four thresholds are physics and astrophysics: [the Big Bang](/citadel/physics/big-bang), [the first stars](/citadel/physics/stellar-astrophysics), [the forging of the elements](/citadel/physics/stellar-astrophysics), and [the formation of planets](/citadel/physics/solar-system-formation). The last four play out on one planet: the origin of life, the rise of intelligence, agriculture and civilisation, and the acceleration to modernity. This post is that Earth-and-human timeline.

## The eight thresholds

1. **The Big Bang** — space, time, energy; inflation; the first particles and light nuclei; the microwave background.
2. **Stars** — gravity collapses gas into stars, ending the cosmic dark ages.
3. **Elements** — stellar fusion and supernovae build carbon, oxygen, iron, and the rest; neutron-star mergers make the heaviest.
4. **Planets** — leftover disk material accretes into worlds, some habitable.
5. **Life** — on Earth, chemistry crosses into self-replication and evolution, ~3.5–4 billion years ago.
6. **Intelligence** — one primate lineage develops language, symbolic thought, and collective learning.
7. **Civilisation** — agriculture allows surplus, settlement, cities, writing, and states.
8. **Modernity** — science and industry accelerate change to an unprecedented rate.

Each threshold builds on the one before, and the gaps between them shrink by orders of magnitude:

## Threshold 5: the Precambrian dawn

Nearly 90% of Earth's history, from formation to the first abundant animals.

![Earth's 4.6-billion-year history drawn as a clock face: the Hadean, Archean, and Proterozoic eons fill almost the whole dial, with the Phanerozoic (Paleozoic, Mesozoic, Cenozoic) squeezed into the last slice; labelled events include the Moon's formation, first life, photosynthesis, the Great Oxidation, Snowball Earth, the Cambrian explosion, the dinosaurs, and the first hominins.](../images/geologic-clock.png "The geologic clock. Complex life occupies only the final sliver of Earth's history. Source: Wikimedia Commons.")

| Eon / Era | Time | Key biological and tectonic events |
|---|---|---|
| **Hadean** | 4.6–4.0 bya | molten surface; the giant impact that formed the Moon; Late Heavy Bombardment; vigorous mantle convection |
| **Archean** | 4.0–2.5 bya | oceans form; earliest chemical traces of life (~3.8 bya) and stromatolites (~3.5 bya) in a low-oxygen atmosphere; first cratons; oxygenic photosynthesis by cyanobacteria begins late; supercontinents Vaalbara, Kenorland |
| **Paleoproterozoic** | 2.5–1.6 bya | the **Great Oxidation Event** (~2.45 bya) — cyanobacterial oxygen builds up, toxic to anaerobes, laying down banded iron formations; first **eukaryotes** (nucleated cells) via endosymbiosis; Huronian glaciations; supercontinent Columbia |
| **Mesoproterozoic** | 1.6–1.0 bya | stable continental platforms; sexual reproduction in eukaryotes; first multicellular algae; supercontinent Rodinia assembles |
| **Neoproterozoic** | 1.0–0.541 bya | Rodinia breaks up; **Snowball Earth** glaciations (Cryogenian); the soft-bodied **Ediacaran biota** — the first clear animals; supercontinent Pannotia |

## Threshold 5 continued: the Phanerozoic

The eon of "visible life" — abundant fossils, and five mass extinctions.

| Period | Time (mya) | Key events |
|---|---|---|
| Cambrian | 541–485 | **Cambrian explosion** — most animal phyla appear with shells and skeletons |
| Ordovician | 485–444 | diverse marine invertebrates; first jawless fish; ends in mass extinction |
| Silurian | 444–419 | first land plants, fungi, and arthropods; first jawed fish; Euramerica forms |
| Devonian | 419–359 | "Age of Fishes"; first amphibians onto land; first forests; Late Devonian extinction |
| Carboniferous | 359–299 | vast coal swamp forests; first reptiles; giant insects; Pangaea begins assembling |
| Permian | 299–252 | mammal ancestors diversify; Pangaea complete; ends in the **Permian–Triassic extinction** ("the Great Dying", ~96% of marine species) |
| Triassic | 252–201 | recovery; first dinosaurs and first mammals; Pangaea starts to rift |
| Jurassic | 201–145 | dinosaurs dominate; first birds; Atlantic opens |
| Cretaceous | 145–66 | flowering plants spread; ends in the **K–Pg extinction** (asteroid impact) that kills the non-avian dinosaurs |

## Threshold 5 continued: the Cenozoic

The "Age of Mammals", 66 mya to now.

| Epoch | Time | Key events |
|---|---|---|
| Paleocene | 66–56 mya | mammals and birds radiate into vacated niches; India–Asia collision begins (Himalayas) |
| Eocene | 56–33.9 mya | warm climate; early primates, whales; grasslands expand |
| Oligocene | 33.9–23 mya | global cooling; Antarctic glaciation |
| Miocene | 23–5.3 mya | apes diversify in Africa; Andes and Rockies rise |
| Pliocene | 5.3–2.6 mya | ice ages begin; hominins (*Australopithecus*) evolve; Panama isthmus closes |
| Pleistocene | 2.6 mya–11,700 ya | repeated glaciations; *Homo erectus*, Neanderthals, *Homo sapiens*; megafauna |
| Holocene | 11,700 ya–now | current interglacial; agriculture and civilisation |


## Thresholds 6–8: the human saga

| Age | Time | Key developments |
|---|---|---|
| Lower Paleolithic | 3.3 Mya–300,000 ya | first stone tools (Lomekwian, Oldowan); *Homo erectus* masters fire, leaves Africa |
| Middle Paleolithic | 300,000–50,000 ya | Mousterian tools; Neanderthals; early *Homo sapiens*; burial, early symbolism |
| Upper Paleolithic | 50,000–12,000 ya | modern humans spread worldwide; complex language, blade and projectile tools; cave art and figurines |
| Mesolithic | ~12,000–10,000 ya | post-Ice-Age adaptation; microliths; bows, canoes |
| Neolithic | ~10,000–4,500 BCE | the **Agricultural Revolution** — plants and animals domesticated independently in several regions; settled villages; pottery |
| Chalcolithic / Bronze Age | ~4500–1200 BCE | copper then bronze; the first cities, states, and writing (Mesopotamia, Egypt, Indus, China) |
| Iron Age | ~1200 BCE– | iron tools and weapons; large empires; alphabetic writing; coinage |
| Classical Antiquity | ~800 BCE–500 CE | Greek and Roman philosophy, mathematics, law, democracy |
| Middle Ages | ~500–1500 CE | European feudalism; the Islamic Golden Age's advances in science and mathematics |
| Renaissance & Age of Discovery | ~1400–1700 CE | printing press; Copernicus and Galileo; global exploration and the Columbian Exchange |
| Scientific Revolution & Enlightenment | ~1550–1800 CE | the scientific method; Newton and Kepler; reason, rights, revolutions |
| Industrial Revolutions | ~1760–1914 | steam, factories, urbanisation; then electricity, steel, mass production |
| Modern era | ~1800–2000 | nation-states, world wars, the nuclear age, the Cold War, decolonisation |
| Digital / Information Age | ~1970–now | computers, the internet, mobile technology, AI; accelerating global change |

The human impact on Earth's geology, climate, and ecosystems since the mid-20th century has led geologists to propose a new epoch, the **Anthropocene**, though it is not yet formally adopted.

## The one idea to keep

Cosmic history is eight thresholds of rising complexity — Big Bang, stars, elements, planets, life, intelligence, civilisation, modernity — each made possible only by the one before, and each arriving faster. The physics thresholds took billions of years; life to intelligence took hundreds of millions; the human thresholds took thousands, then hundreds. On Earth specifically, the pattern is oxygen (photosynthesis rewriting the atmosphere and enabling complex cells), then the Cambrian explosion, then five mass extinctions that each reset and redirected the biosphere, then one primate lineage whose collective learning compounds change on a timescale of decades. The acceleration has not stopped.
